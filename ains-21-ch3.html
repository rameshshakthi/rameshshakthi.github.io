Chapter 03

Measuring the Financial Performance of Insurers

Underneath the accounting language, this chapter asks one question: is the insurer financially healthy enough to keep operating and pay its claims? That splits into two ideas — profitability ("did we earn?") and solvency ("can we meet our obligations?") — and a small set of formulas that measure both.

01 — The Real Question

Two big ideas: profitability & solvency

Don't let the accounting language distract you — Chapter 3 is really asking one thing: "Is the insurance company financially healthy enough to keep operating and pay its claims?"

INSURER FINANCIAL HEALTH
Profitability"Are we earning?"
Solvency"Can we pay what we owe?"

Insurers use financial statements to monitor and report performance — and regulators, insureds, investors, agents, and brokers all rely on that same information to evaluate an insurer.

02 — The Cash Flow

The insurer money machine

The central principle

Revenue must ultimately be sufficient to cover losses and expenses if the insurer is to remain financially viable. Underwriting income and investment income are the two major sources of insurer income.

03 — Related, Not the Same

Profitability vs. solvency

ProfitabilitySolvency
"Did the insurer make money?""Does the insurer have enough financial strength to meet its obligations?"
Revenue > expenses = profit; revenue < expenses = lossAn insurer can have a bad year and still be financially strong
Example

Jai's insurer has strong assets, a large policyholders' surplus, good liquidity — and one bad underwriting year. It may still be financially healthy. A company can even show a profit in one period while carrying serious underlying problems.

Exam distinction

Profitability = performance over a period. Solvency = financial condition, the ability to meet obligations.

04 — Where Income Comes From

Underwriting income & investment income

Underwriting income

The amount remaining after underwriting losses and expenses are subtracted from premiums — premiums are the revenue from underwriting operations.

Net Underwriting Gain/Loss = Earned Premiums − Losses − Underwriting Expenses

Investment income

Insurers collect premiums before they necessarily need to pay all claims, so those funds can be invested in the meantime.

Customer pays premium
↓
Insurer receives cash; claim may occur later
↓
Money is invested meanwhile → investment income
Example

Ramesh buys a one-year policy and pays ₹20,000. The insurer doesn't necessarily pay a ₹20,000 claim tomorrow — until funds are needed, it can invest them and earn investment income. Investment strategy has to balance return + safety + availability of funds, since the insurer needs investments that are relatively secure and readily convertible to cash to meet claim obligations.

05 — The Core Premium Concept

Written, earned & unearned premium

Written premiumEarned premiumUnearned premium
Premium on policies put into effect during a specified periodThe portion of written premium for the part of the policy period already elapsedThe portion for coverage not yet provided — money received, but future protection still owed
Example — Jai's ₹600 policy

Jai buys a one-year policy effective July 1 for ₹600. On July 1, the full ₹600 is written — but the insurer hasn't earned all of it, because six more months of coverage are still owed into next year.

Earned = ₹600 × 6/12 = ₹300 · Unearned = ₹600 × 6/12 = ₹300
The magazine subscription trick

Sabari pays ₹1,200 for a 12-month magazine subscription and receives 3 issues. Earned = ₹300 (3/12 received); unearned = ₹900 (9/12 still owed). Coverage already provided = earned. Coverage still to be provided = unearned.

Exam trap

Written asks "what policies/premiums were written this period?" Earned asks "how much premium relates to coverage already provided?" Memory: WRITTEN = policy started. EARNED = coverage provided.

06 — Claims, Two Ways

Paid vs. incurred losses

A paid loss is a claim payment the insurer has actually made — definite, because it's already happened.

Example

Ramesh's covered claim is settled for ₹80,000 → paid loss = ₹80,000.

Why paid loss isn't enough

Suppose an insurer has ₹80,000 in paid claims, ₹100,000 in claims still being investigated, and ₹50,000 in IBNR. Looking only at paid losses gives an incomplete picture — the insurer must estimate obligations from losses that occurred but aren't fully settled yet.

07 — Money Set Aside

Loss reserve

A loss reserve is money estimated and set aside to pay claims for losses that have already occurred but aren't yet settled.

Loss occurred → claim open
↓
Estimated future payment
↓
Loss reserve
Example

Mari has an open liability claim. The insurer estimates the eventual settlement at ₹5,00,000 and establishes a reserve for it.

08 — The Silent Loss

IBNR

IBNR = Incurred But Not Reported — losses that have occurred but haven't yet been reported to the insurer.

Example

A storm damages Sabari's warehouse on December 30, but he doesn't report it until January 3. At December 31: loss occurred = yes, claim reported = no. That loss falls into IBNR.

Memory

IBNR = happened, but the insurer hasn't been told yet.

09 — Putting Losses Together

The incurred losses formula

Change in Loss Reserves = Ending Reserve − Beginning Reserve
Incurred Losses = Paid Losses + Change in Loss Reserves
Example — Ramesh's insurer

Paid losses = ₹400,000 · Beginning reserve = ₹300,000 · Ending reserve = ₹500,000.
Change in reserve = ₹500,000 − ₹300,000 = ₹200,000.
Incurred losses = ₹400,000 + ₹200,000 = ₹600,000.

10 — The Cost of Settling

Loss adjustment expenses

Insurers incur costs while investigating and settling claims: investigation, legal defense, claim evaluation, adjuster expenses, settlement administration.

Investigate accident
↓
Hire experts, evaluate legal responsibility
↓
Defend lawsuit, negotiate settlement

Liability claims especially can produce substantial loss adjustment expenses, since legal responsibility often requires complex investigation and defense — as in Jai's business facing a liability lawsuit.

12 — Investing Isn't Free

Investment expenses

Investment departments have their own costs too — staff salaries, administration, and other operating costs.

Net Investment Income = Investment Income − Investment Expenses

Investment income is never simply the gross return an insurer's investments earned.

13 — Underwriting's Bottom Line

Net underwriting gain/loss

Net Underwriting Gain/Loss = Earned Premiums − Losses − Underwriting Expenses
Example — Sabari's insurer

Earned premiums ₹10 crore − incurred losses ₹7 crore − underwriting expenses ₹2 crore = ₹1 crore underwriting gain.

15 — Textbook Case

Worked example: follow the money (Atwell Insurer)

Underwriting result = $1,000,000 − $1,080,000 = −$80,000
Investment income = +$100,000
Overall result = −$80,000 + $100,000 = +$20,000
Lesson

An insurer can lose money on underwriting but still produce an overall gain because of investment income.

16 — Shifting to Financial Position

Assets, liabilities, surplus

Now we move from "did the insurer make money?" to "what does the insurer own and owe?"

Assets − Liabilities = Policyholders' Surplus
17 — What the Insurer Owns

Assets

Property or resources owned by the insurer: cash, stocks, bonds, real estate, equipment, receivables.

18 — A Regulator's Filter

Admitted vs. non-admitted assets

Admitted assetsNon-admitted assets
Regulators allow these on the financial statement — generally readily convertible to cash at or near market valueRegulators exclude these because they may not be readily convertible to cash at or near market value
Cash, stocks, bonds, mortgages, real estate, certain computer equipment, premium balances under 90 daysCertain office equipment, furniture and supplies, premiums over 90 days overdue
Memory

Admitted = regulator lets it count. Non-admitted = regulator doesn't — reflecting a conservative approach to evaluating insurer financial strength.

20 — The Financial Cushion

Policyholders' surplus

Policyholders' Surplus = Admitted Assets − Liabilities
Example — Ramesh's insurer

Admitted assets ₹100 crore − liabilities ₹70 crore = ₹30 crore surplus.

Unexpected losses exceed expectations
↓
Insurer draws on surplus
↓
Claims can still be paid

Policyholders' surplus is a cushion against adverse financial experience, and a key measure of financial condition.

22 — Why Numbers Need Context

Why ratios matter

Two insurers both saying "we made ₹10 crore" tells you very little — that could be excellent for a tiny insurer and disappointing for a giant one. Ratios turn raw numbers into a comparison that means something.

23 — Ratio 1

Loss ratio

Loss Ratio = Incurred Losses (incl. LAE) ÷ Earned Premiums
Example

₹75,00,000 ÷ ₹1,00,00,000 = 75% — about 75% of earned premium is being consumed by losses and their settlement.

24 — Ratio 2

Expense ratio

Expense Ratio = Incurred Underwriting Expenses ÷ Written Premiums
Example

₹33,00,000 ÷ ₹1,10,00,000 = 30% of written premiums used for underwriting expenses.

Exam trap

Loss ratio → earned premium. Expense ratio → written premium. This denominator difference is a favorite trap.

25 — Ratio 3

Dividend ratio

Dividend Ratio = Policyholder Dividends ÷ Earned Premiums

Example: ₹10,000 ÷ ₹10,00,000 = 1%. It isn't a profitability measure on its own, but it can be folded into the combined ratio after dividends.

26 — The Signature Formula

Combined ratio

Combined Ratio = Loss Ratio + Expense Ratio
Combined Ratio after Dividends = Loss Ratio + Expense Ratio + Dividend Ratio
Example

Loss ratio 75% + expense ratio 30% = combined ratio 105%.

Combined ratioMeaning
< 100%Underwriting gain
= 100%Underwriting break-even
> 100%Underwriting loss

Many insurers run combined ratios above 100% and offset the underwriting loss with investment income — so a lower combined ratio is generally better, but remember it does not include investment income, so it never tells the whole profitability story on its own.

27 — Ratio 4

Investment income ratio

Investment Income Ratio = Net Investment Income ÷ Earned Premiums

Example: ₹10 lakh ÷ ₹1 crore = 10%. It measures how successful investment activity has been relative to earned premiums.

28 — The Most Complete Ratio

Overall operating ratio

Overall Operating Ratio = Combined Ratio − Investment Income Ratio
Example — Sabari's insurer, the complete story

Loss ratio 75% + expense ratio 30% = combined ratio 105%. Investment income ratio 10%. Overall operating ratio = 105% − 10% = 95%.
Underwriting: bad (105%). Investments: helpful (10%). Overall: good (95%) — this is why looking only at underwriting can be misleading.

Overall operating ratioMeaning
< 100%Overall operating gain
= 100%Break-even
> 100%Overall operating loss

This is considered the most complete of the commonly used ratios, because it factors in both underwriting and investment results.

29 — Business vs. Cushion

Capacity ratio

Capacity Ratio (premium-to-surplus) = Written Premiums ÷ Policyholders' Surplus
Example

₹11 crore ÷ ₹5 crore = 2.2 : 1 — comparing business being written against the financial cushion available to absorb adverse results. An excessively high ratio may signal that an insurer's cushion is too small for its exposure, though regulators weigh many measures, not this one alone.

Chapter 3 Wrap-Up

Formula cheat sheet & one-minute revision

Must know cold

Change in Reserve = Ending − Beginning
Incurred Losses = Paid + Change in Reserves
Net Investment Income = Investment Income − Investment Expenses
Net Underwriting Gain/Loss = Earned Premiums − Losses − Underwriting Expenses
Overall Gain/Loss = Net Underwriting + Net Investment Gain/Loss
Loss Ratio = Incurred Losses (incl. LAE) ÷ Earned Premiums
Expense Ratio = Underwriting Expenses ÷ Written Premiums
Combined Ratio = Loss Ratio + Expense Ratio
Investment Income Ratio = Net Investment Income ÷ Earned Premiums
Overall Operating Ratio = Combined Ratio − Investment Income Ratio
Policyholders' Surplus = Admitted Assets − Liabilities
Capacity Ratio = Written Premiums ÷ Policyholders' Surplus

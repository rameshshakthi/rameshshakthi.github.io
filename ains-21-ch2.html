Chapter 02

Who Provides Insurance & How Is It Regulated?

Insurance is sold by several very different kinds of organizations — stock companies, mutuals, reciprocals, Lloyd's, captives, reinsurers — and by government when the private market can't reach an exposure economically. Every one of them answers, in some form, to a state insurance department. This chapter is written around the U.S. system, so terms like NAIC, FAIR plans, and IRIS should be read in that context.

34 — Classic Exam Trap

Lloyd's

Lloyd's is NOT technically an insurer

The textbook describes Lloyd's as an association / marketplace that provides facilities and procedures for its members to write insurance.

LLOYD'S — "the marketplace"
Syndicate A
underwriters
Syndicate B
underwriters

Individual investor-members are called Names. Names belong to syndicates, and syndicate underwriters conduct the actual insurance operations and evaluate applications.

Memorize instead

Lloyd's = insurance marketplace / association, not an insurance company.

35 — The Smaller Cousin

American Lloyd's

American Lloyd's are much smaller than Lloyd's, and most are domiciled in Texas. Their underwriters have limited liability to their investment in the association, and they generally operate as a single syndicate under an attorney-in-fact.

Major insurance marketplace / associationSmaller associations
Members include investors (Names)Underwriters have limited liability

36 — In-House Insurer

Captive insurer

A captive insurer is a subsidiary created by a company (or affiliated group) to provide insurance for its parent or the group.

Parent company owns the subsidiary
↓
Captive insurer
↓
Provides insurance back to the parent / group

Three reasons companies create captives:

  1. 1Lower insurance cost
  2. 2Insurance availability
  3. 3Improved cash flow
Example

Sabari's large business has difficult-to-insure risks. Instead of buying all coverage from an unrelated insurer, it creates a captive subsidiary — premiums stay within the corporate structure until a claim needs paying.

37 — Insurance for the Insurer

Example

44 — Coordinator, Not Regulator

NAIC

NAIC = National Association of Insurance Commissioners. It encourages coordination, cooperation, and uniformity among state insurance departments.

45 — Don't Confuse Rate & Premium

Ratemaking

TermMeaning
RateAmount charged per exposure unit
PremiumPrice of insurance coverage for the specified period
Formula

Premium = Rate × Number of exposure units
Example: rate = ₹1.20 per ₹100 of coverage, coverage = ₹1,000 → 10 exposure units → premium = ₹1.20 × 10 = ₹12.

47 — Six Systems

Types of insurance rating laws

These range along a ladder of how much regulator involvement is required before rates go into effect.

More regulator involvement
Prior approval — file, then wait for approval before use
Flex rating — prior approval only if the change exceeds a set range
File-and-use — file first, then use without waiting
Use-and-file — use first, then file within a set period
Open competition — no prior filing requirement
State-mandated rates — the state sets the rate directly
Less prior regulator involvement
48 — Watching Financial Strength

Solvency surveillance

Solvency means the insurer can meet its financial obligations as they become due and remain financially viable. Two major tools: insurer examinations and IRIS.

49 — The Health Check

Insurer examination

Regulators periodically examine insurers, reviewing claims, underwriting, marketing, accounting, and financial condition.

Insurance Department examines the insurer
↓
Claims + Underwriting + Marketing + Accounting + Financial condition
↓
Problems found? → Corrective action. None found? → Continue monitoring
50 — Early Warning

IRIS

IRIS = Insurance Regulatory Information System — an early-warning system operated by the NAIC. It uses financial-statement data to calculate 12 financial ratios and flags insurers whose results fall outside predetermined norms for regulatory attention.

Memory

IRIS = financial early-warning system. It is not a claims system.

52 — A Common Terminology Trap

Domestic, foreign, and alien insurers

Classification is based on where the insurer is incorporated, relative to the state where it operates.

TermIncorporated in
DomesticThe same U.S. state where it's operating
ForeignAnother U.S. state
AlienAnother country
Example

An insurer incorporated in Massachusetts is domestic when operating in Massachusetts, foreign when operating in New Hampshire, and alien if it were instead incorporated in London.

53 — Admitted to Operate

Licensing insurers

Most insurers must be licensed by the state insurance department before writing insurance there. A licensed insurer is also called an admitted insurer. The state may suspend or revoke a license when requirements aren't met.

54 — Agents, Brokers, Adjusters

Licensing insurance representatives

States also license insurance representatives — agents, brokers, and in some states, claim representatives/adjusters — often with continuing education requirements.

55 — Clearer Contracts

Policy form approval

Many states require insurers to file policy forms with the insurance department, to reduce unfair or unreasonable provisions and misleading wording.

56 — Fair Dealing

Market conduct

Market conduct regulation focuses on insurer practices in sales, underwriting, claims, and bad-faith actions. If an insurer systematically uses unfair claims practices, regulators can investigate and act.

61 — Still Regulated

E&S regulation

E&S insurers are often non-admitted. They have more flexibility on rate and form filing than standard insurers, but the E&S market is still regulated — in many states, E&S business must be placed through an E&S/surplus lines broker.

Memory

Non-admitted does not mean "unregulated."

High-Value Exam Traps

Ten traps to watch for

Trap 1

✗ Insurance = risk management.

✓ Insurance is one risk-management technique; it's also a transfer system, a business, and a contract.

Trap 2

✗ A loss exposure means a loss has already happened.

✓ A loss exposure means there is a possibility of loss.

Trap 3

✗ Pure risk allows gain.

✓ Pure risk = loss or no loss. Speculative risk = possibility of gain and/or loss.

Trap 4

✗ Lloyd's is an insurer.

✓ Lloyd's is not technically an insurer — it's an association/marketplace facilitating insurance written by its members.

Trap 5

✗ A mutual insurer is owned by stockholders.

✓ A mutual insurer is owned by policyholders.

Trap 6

✗ A reciprocal is a stock company.

✓ A reciprocal is an unincorporated association of subscribers, run by an attorney-in-fact.

Trap 7

✗ Reinsurance protects the policyholder directly.

✓ The primary insurer transfers some risk to the reinsurer — reinsurance mainly manages the insurer's own exposure.

Trap 8

✗ NAIC directly regulates insurers.

✓ NAIC coordinates state regulators and develops model laws — it has no direct regulatory authority.

Trap 9

✗ A foreign insurer is incorporated in a foreign country.

✓ Foreign = incorporated in another U.S. state. Alien = incorporated in another country.

Trap 10

✗ Non-admitted means unregulated.

✓ E&S / non-admitted insurance is still subject to applicable regulation.

The one story to keep in your head

Ramesh, Jai, Mari, Sabari, and Sivaprakasam all face loss exposures. Some risks get avoided or controlled; some get transferred through insurance. The insurer pools premiums from many insureds and uses the law of large numbers to estimate losses, then markets → underwrites → prices → handles claims. Different organizations provide that insurance — stock → mutual → reciprocal → Lloyd's → captive → reinsurance — with government stepping in where the private market can't reach. Regulators watch it all: rates → solvency → consumer protection. That's the skeleton of Chapters 1 and 2.

Chapter 05

Underwriting

Underwriting is the insurer's risk-selection and risk-pricing job — deciding who to cover, what to charge, and on what terms, then watching whether the decision holds up over time. This chapter covers adverse selection, capacity, reinsurance, the four hazards, and how an application becomes an accepted, priced, and monitored policy.

01 — The Insurer's Filter

What is underwriting?

Underwriting is the process of selecting insureds, pricing coverage, determining policy terms and conditions, and then monitoring how those decisions actually perform. For every applicant, the insurer works through the same chain of questions.

Who is the applicant?
↓
What can go wrong?
↓
How likely is it?
↓
How severe could it be?
↓
Can we accept it — and at what price?
↓
What conditions apply?
↓
Monitor the result
Example

Mari owns a small warehouse. Jai, her agent, sends her application to Ramesh, the underwriter. Ramesh finds combustible materials stored on site, weak fire protection, old wiring, and a history of frequent past claims. He doesn't reject the account outright — he says "we can insure this if the electrical system and fire protection are improved." That's underwriting in action: not a simple yes or no, but a reasoned judgment about risk, price, and conditions.

02 — Why It Matters

Why underwriting matters

Insurance works by pooling many risks together. If an insurer accepts too many poor risks at inadequate prices, the pool itself becomes unstable.

Poor selection
→
Higher-than-expected losses
→
Bad underwriting results
→
Financial pressure

Good underwriting is not about avoiding claims — claims are expected, and paying them is the insurer's core promise. It's about selecting and pricing risks so the insurer can reasonably meet expected losses, expenses, and profit objectives.

03 — Four Jobs, One Discipline

The four underwriting activities

  1. 1Selecting insureds — deciding which risks fit the insurer's appetite
  2. 2Pricing coverage — determining a premium appropriate to the exposure
  3. 3Determining terms and conditions — limits, deductibles, exclusions, coverage terms, and required loss-control measures
  4. 4Monitoring underwriting decisions — underwriting continues after the policy is issued; the insurer keeps asking "did the risk perform as expected?"
04 — Who Wants In

Adverse selection

Adverse selection occurs when people who are more likely to experience losses are more motivated to obtain insurance. Left unmanaged, this skews the insurer's book toward exactly the risks it can least afford.

ApplicantFlood exposureMotivation to buy
House A — on a hilltopLowLow urgency
House B — next to a riverHighStrong urgency

If the insurer accepts too many high-risk applicants like House B without properly pricing them, the result is a high-risk book, more claims, and premiums that turn out to be inadequate.

Memory — the burning building

A building is already on fire and the owner suddenly wants to buy fire insurance. The insurer refuses, because the probability of loss is now extremely high. Memory: adverse selection means high-risk people have the strongest motivation to buy insurance — which is exactly why underwriting has to screen for it.

05 — The Fuel Tank

Capacity

Capacity is the amount of business an insurer is able to write — its risk-writing "fuel tank."

Financial resources + policyholders' surplus + reinsurance + expertise/resources
↓
Capacity
↓
How much business can safely be written

Capacity can be affected by:

  • Financial resources
  • Underwriting resources
  • Claim-handling resources
  • Geographic concentration
  • Reinsurance availability
  • Expertise
Example

Sivaprakasam tells Ramesh, "we already have a large concentration of coastal property risk — we should not keep adding unlimited exposure in the same area." That's a capacity decision: it isn't about any single risk being bad, it's about how much of one kind of risk the insurer can safely hold.

06 — Insurance for Insurers

Reinsurance

Reinsurance is insurance for insurers — it lets a primary insurer transfer part of its risk to another company.

Policyholder
→
Primary insurer
→
Transfers part of the risk
→
Reinsurer
TypeHow it works
Treaty reinsuranceA group/automatic arrangement — many eligible policies fall under a treaty, and the reinsurer automatically participates in all of them
Facultative reinsuranceAn individual risk considered separately — a large or unusual risk is submitted on its own, and the reinsurer accepts or rejects it case by case
Memory

Treaty = automatic group arrangement. Facultative = individual negotiation.

08 — Frequency vs. Severity

Loss prevention vs. loss reduction

ConceptEffectExample
Loss preventionReduces frequencyRegular vehicle maintenance → fewer accidents caused by mechanical failure
Loss reductionReduces severityA fire starts → a sprinkler activates → fire damage is limited
Memory

Prevention = stop the loss from happening. Reduction = reduce the damage once it does.

10 — Three Ways Forward

The underwriting decision

Once Ramesh has the information and understands the hazards, he has three broad choices.

APPLICATION

Example

Mari's bookstore has poor fire protection. Ramesh says, "we can cover it if you install an appropriate fire alarm system" — the risk becomes acceptable once the condition is met.

11 — What It Costs

Pricing

Premium = Rate × Exposure Units

An exposure unit measures the loss exposure being insured:

  • Workers' compensation — each ₹100 (or $100) of payroll
  • Property insurance — each ₹100 (or $100) of insurance
  • Auto liability — each car-month insured
Worked example

Rate = ₹100 per car-month. One car is insured for 12 months, so there are 12 car-months of exposure.

₹100 × 12 car-months = ₹1,200 premium

Exam Traps

The most important Chapter 5 traps

Trap 1

✗ Adverse selection = fraud.

✓ Not necessarily — it's about high-risk individuals being more motivated to seek insurance.

Trap 2

✗ Treaty = individual negotiation.

✓ Treaty generally covers a defined class of eligible business automatically.

Trap 3

✗ Facultative = automatic.

✓ Facultative is individually considered.

Trap 4

✗ Physical hazard = intentional behavior.

✓ Physical hazard is a tangible condition, not intent.

Chapter 09

Liability Loss Exposures & Policy Provisions

Liability insurance protects against legal responsibility to someone else — a very different animal from insuring your own property. This chapter covers the legal bases for a claim, the major liability exposure categories, and how policies define covered parties, activities, time period, and limits.

01 — The Starting Point

What is a liability loss exposure?

A liability loss exposure exists when an individual or organization could become legally responsible for injury or damage to another party. It involves two things at once: the legal basis for a claim against you, and the potential financial consequences if that claim succeeds.

02 — Two Very Different Animals

Property vs. liability

It's easy to blur these together, but they protect against opposite directions of loss.

Key difference

Property insurance protects the insured's own property. Liability insurance protects against covered legal responsibility to others.

03 — Five Ways to Be Liable

Legal foundations

A liability claim has to rest on some legal basis. AINS-21 recognizes five:

  1. 1Negligence
  2. 2Intentional tort
  3. 3Strict liability
  4. 4Breach of contract
  5. 5Statutory liability
04 — Failing to Take Care

Negligence

Generally, negligence means failing to use the degree of care a reasonable person would use in the circumstances, resulting in harm to someone else.

Example

Jai owns a store. He knows a section of floor is wet but does nothing about it. Mari enters the store, slips, and is injured.

Danger exists (wet floor)
↓
Jai fails to act reasonably
↓
Mari is injured
↓
Possible negligence claim
05 — On Purpose

Intentional tort

An intentional tort is an intentional act that causes legally recognized harm to someone else — the harm doesn't have to be intended, but the underlying act does.

Key distinction

Negligence = lack of the required degree of care. Intentional tort = intentional conduct.

06 — Liability Without Proving Fault the Usual Way

Strict liability

Strict liability can impose responsibility without requiring the claimant to prove negligence in the usual way. It's connected to inherently dangerous activities and to statutory situations — such as workers' compensation.

07 — A Statutory Example

Workers' compensation example

Workers' compensation creates a statutory system for work-related injuries. Employees receive specified benefits for covered work-related injuries regardless of fault, under the statutory system — no need to prove the employer was negligent.

09 — Danger on the Property

Premises liability

If someone is injured because of a dangerous condition on a property, the owner or occupier may face liability.

Example

A visitor slips on an unsafe surface in Mari's store.

Premises condition

10 — During the Job, and After It

Business operations & completed operations

Business operations

A business can create liability through what it does — even away from its own premises.

Example

A plumber accidentally starts a fire while working at a customer's house. The exposure arises from the operation itself, not from the plumber's own premises.

Completed operations

The job may be finished, but the liability exposure can continue.

Example

An electrician completes wiring work. Months later, faulty wiring causes a fire.

Work completed
↓
Defect remains
↓
Later injury or damage
↓
Completed operations exposure
11 — Liability After the Sale

Products liability

Products can create liability after they're sold — arising from design, manufacture, distribution, or sale/use. Product liability exposure can begin as early as the product's design stage and continue until proper disposal by the ultimate consumer.

12 — Liability Through Promotion

Advertising liability

Businesses can create liability through their advertising activities. Examples include:

  • Invasion of privacy
  • Improper use of another company's trademark or advertising material
13 — Contamination and Cost

Pollution liability

Pollution can create cleanup costs, injury, property damage, relocation expenses, and legal defense costs. Industrial contamination is an example of a potentially enormous liability exposure.

17 — What a Liability Policy Must Establish

Liability policy provisions

A liability policy needs to establish six things:

  1. 1Covered parties
  2. 2Covered activities
  3. 3Covered injury or damage
  4. 4Covered costs
  5. 5Covered time period
  6. 6Factors affecting claim payment

Covered parties

The named insured, and potentially other insured persons, depending on their relationship to the named insured and the circumstances of the claim.

Covered activities

Auto liability, for example, may focus on covered auto accidents. General liability can cover many business activities, subject to exclusions.

Covered injury or damage

Bodily injury, property damage, personal injury, advertising injury.

18 — When Does Coverage Trigger?

Covered time period: occurrence vs. claims-made

BasisCoverage is generally triggered by
Occurrence basisWhen the event causing injury or damage occurs during the policy period
Claims-made basisWhen the claim is made during the policy period, subject to the policy's requirements
Memory

Occurrence → when the event occurs. Claims-made → when the claim is made.

19 — One Bucket or Two?

Split limits vs. single limit

StructureHow it works
Split limitsSeparate limits for different categories — e.g. Bodily injury: ₹X per person / ₹Y per occurrence; Property damage: ₹Z per occurrence
Single limitOne limit applies to a combination of bodily injury and property damage from the same occurrence
Memory

Split = separate buckets. Single = one bucket.

Many liability policies provide defense costs in addition to damages — but policy wording controls. Some policies place defense costs within the overall limit, so always read the provision.

Also worth knowing

Two or more liability policies may respond to the same claim. Other-insurance provisions help determine how the policies share responsibility.

4 Exam Traps

The most important Chapter 9 traps

Trap 1

✗ Liability only means bodily injury.

✓ It can involve property damage and other covered injury categories, depending on policy.

Trap 2

✗ A liability claim always requires negligence.

✓ Other legal bases include intentional tort, strict liability, contract, and statute.

Trap 3

✗ Occurrence and claims-made mean the same thing.

✓ Occurrence → event occurs during the policy period. Claims-made → claim made during the policy period.

Trap 4

✗ Defense costs are always inside the policy limit.

✓ Not necessarily — policy wording controls.

Chapter 08

Property Loss Exposures & Policy Provisions

A property loss exposure comes down to three questions: what property, what can damage it, and what financial consequence follows. This chapter covers perils, valuation, policy limits, deductibles, coinsurance and insurance-to-value, and other-insurance provisions — anchored in Mari's supermarket fire.

01 — The Three Questions

Start with three questions

Every property loss exposure comes down to the same three questions, and Chapter 8 is organized around them. Ask what property is exposed, what can damage it, and what financial consequences follow — and the rest of the chapter's provisions (valuation, limits, deductibles, coinsurance, other insurance) simply describe how a policy answers the third question.

What property?buildings, contents, vehicles, and more
↓
What can damage it?the peril — fire, theft, collision, flood
↓
What financial consequences result?reduced value, lost income, extra expense
02 — What Counts As Property

Types of property

Property loss exposures don't stop at "the building." Insurers organize property into several recognized types, because each behaves differently in terms of valuation, risk, and coverage design.

  1. 1Buildings
  2. 2Personal property / contents
  3. 3Money and securities
  4. 4Motor vehicles and trailers
  5. 5Property in transit
  6. 6Ships and cargo
  7. 7Boilers and machinery

Building

A building is more than its walls and roof. It can include plumbing, wiring, heating and air-conditioning systems, permanently attached equipment, elevators, built-in appliances, and certain building-service equipment.

Contents

Contents are the personal property inside — furniture, electronics, machinery, equipment, stock, and inventory are typical examples.

03 — From Peril To Payment

The property loss chain

A property loss doesn't start with a check being written — it starts with a peril acting on property, which causes damage, which creates a financial consequence, which the insurance policy then responds to.

Example

Mari's store has an electrical problem, which starts a fire. The fire damages the building and the inventory. The store has to shut down, so Mari loses income and incurs extra expenses to keep serving customers — each step in the chain, from peril to financial consequence, is a distinct piece of the exposure.

04 — Cause Of Loss

Perils, a quick recap

A peril is a cause of loss — fire, theft, collision, and flood are all perils. How a policy lists its perils determines how broadly it responds.

ApproachHow it works
Named perilsThe policy lists the specific causes of loss it covers — anything not listed is not covered
Special formThe policy generally covers direct loss from any cause, unless an exclusion applies
05 — Three Categories

Financial consequences of property loss

A property loss can create financial consequences in three broad categories.

  1. 1Reduced property value
  2. 2Lost income
  3. 3Extra expenses

A supermarket burns. Building damage, contents damage, lost income while closed, and extra expenses to operate from a temporary location all add up to the total financial impact of the single fire.

06 — The Ceiling, Not The Guarantee

Policy limits

A policy limit is the maximum amount of coverage available for a particular type of loss, subject to the policy's other provisions. A policy limit is a ceiling — it is not a promise that every covered loss produces a payment of that size.

Example

A policy carries a ₹1 crore limit. A covered loss occurs — but that does not automatically mean a ₹1 crore payment. The actual payment depends on several things.

  1. 1The actual covered loss
  2. 2Valuation
  3. 3Deductible
  4. 4Exclusions
  5. 5Other policy provisions
  6. 6Applicable limits
07 — What Is It Worth

Valuation provisions

A valuation provision tells you how a covered loss is measured in money. Three approaches are common.

ApproachMeaning
Replacement costThe cost to replace the damaged property with new property of like kind and quality, with no deduction for depreciation
Actual cash value (ACV)Replacement cost minus depreciation
Agreed valueA value the insurer and insured agree to in advance, used to settle a covered total loss
Replacement Cost − Depreciation = Actual Cash Value
Example

A new machine costs ₹20 lakh to replace. Depreciation is ₹6 lakh. ACV = ₹20 lakh − ₹6 lakh = ₹14 lakh.

08 — How The Insurer Pays

Settlement options

Once a loss is valued, the policy generally gives the insurer a choice among general settlement options.

  1. 1Pay the value determined under the valuation provision
  2. 2Pay the cost to repair or replace the damaged property
  3. 3Repair, rebuild, or replace the property with property of like kind and quality

09 — Sharing The First Loss

Deductibles

A deductible is the amount the insured absorbs before the insurer's payment begins.

Covered loss
↓
minus deductible
↓

Deductibles exist for good reasons.

  1. 1Discourages careless behavior
  2. 2Reduces small claims
  3. 3Reduces administrative costs
  4. 4Helps control premiums
10 — Insuring Close To Value

Insurance-to-value and coinsurance

Insurers want property insured close to its actual value. If an owner insures only a fraction of a property's worth but can still collect in full for a partial loss, that owner effectively gets full protection for a fraction of the premium — which is unfair to properly insured policyholders and to the insurer's rate structure.

Example, without coinsurance

A building is worth ₹4 crore, but the owner only insures it for ₹2 crore. A ₹2 crore loss occurs. Without an insurance-to-value provision, the owner could potentially recover the full ₹2 crore loss despite having insured only half the building's value.

A coinsurance provision addresses this by reducing the payment when property is underinsured relative to a stated percentage requirement.

(Insurance Carried ÷ Insurance Required) × Loss = Payment before deductible/limit
Worked example

Building value ₹1 crore, coinsurance requirement 80% → required insurance = ₹80 lakh. Insurance carried = ₹40 lakh. Covered loss = ₹20 lakh.
(₹40 lakh ÷ ₹80 lakh) × ₹20 lakh = ₹10 lakh — then the applicable deductible and policy terms are considered.

Exam warning

Do not automatically apply a coinsurance formula unless the question gives a coinsurance requirement and the policy context supports it.

11 — When Two Policies Respond

Other insurance

Sometimes the same property is covered by more than one policy. An insured shouldn't profit simply because two policies both respond to the same loss — other-insurance provisions allocate payment between the insurers and help preserve indemnity (restoring the insured, not enriching them).

12 — Case Study

Mari's supermarket fire

Mari's Supermarket — the whole chapter in one story

Mari owns a supermarket with a building, stock, freezers, cash registers, and a business income exposure. A fire starts in the storage area: an electrical problem ignites a fire, which damages the building and the contents. The store has to close — producing lost income and extra expenses on top of the physical damage — and Mari files claims for each piece of the loss.

Because Mari had previously identified these exposures and arranged appropriate coverage — the right property, valued the right way, with limits, deductibles, and other-insurance provisions she understood — she is able to rebuild and reopen. That is the central lesson of the chapter: identifying the exposure and matching it with the right provisions is what turns a fire into a recoverable event instead of a business-ending one.

4 Exam Traps

The most important Chapter 8 traps

Trap 1

✗ Policy limit = automatic payment.

✓ The actual payment depends on the covered loss and the policy's provisions.

Trap 2

✗ Named perils means everything is covered except exclusions.

✓ Named perils means only the listed causes of loss are covered.

Trap 3

✗ Special form means every loss is covered.

✓ Exclusions still apply under a special form policy.

Trap 4

✗ Coinsurance is just another deductible.

✓ Coinsurance addresses the relationship between insurance carried and insurance required/value — a deductible is a separate, flat sharing of the first loss.

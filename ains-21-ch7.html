Chapter 07

Insurance Contracts

Insurance is a contract like any other, built from the same four elements as any binding agreement — but it also carries six special characteristics that exist because one side is a promise about an uncertain future. This chapter covers those elements, the anatomy of a policy, and how endorsements and cancellation work.

01 — A Promise, Not a Product on a Shelf

Why insurance is a special contract

When Mari buys a laptop, she can inspect it, turn it on, and see exactly what she's getting before she pays. When she buys insurance, she's buying something very different — a promise about what will happen if an uncertain future event occurs. There's nothing to hold, weigh, or test on the spot.

The policy itself is the evidence of that promise. It's a written contract that defines the rights and duties of both the insurer and the insured — what each side must do, and what each side can expect from the other, for as long as the agreement is in force.

Memory

Ordinary purchase = inspect now, get it now. Insurance purchase = pay now, the promise is tested only if a covered loss happens later.

02 — A-C-L-C

Four essential elements of a valid contract

Like any binding agreement, an insurance contract needs four essential elements. Without any one of them, there's no valid contract at all — insurance or otherwise.

Agreement

Agreement requires an offer and an acceptance. Typically, Mari completes an application — that's her offer to buy insurance — and the insurer's acceptance of that offer creates the agreement.

Watch for modification

If Ramesh, underwriting the application, offers to issue the policy only with a higher deductible than Mari requested, that's not acceptance of her offer — it's a counteroffer. There's no agreement until Mari, in turn, accepts Ramesh's modified terms.

Competent parties

Both parties must have the legal capacity to enter into a contract. This can relate to mental capacity, intoxication, and age (a minor's capacity to contract may be limited), as well as to whether the insurer itself is properly licensed to transact insurance business in the first place.

Legal purpose

The contract's purpose must be lawful. An insurance contract can't validly exist to insure an illegal subject matter simply because someone wants the coverage — the underlying purpose has to be legal for the contract to be valid.

Consideration

Each side must give something of value. Mari's consideration is her payment, or promise to pay, the premium; the insurer's consideration is its promise to pay for a covered loss.

04 — Characteristics 1 & 2

Conditional & fortuitous events

Conditional contract

The insurer's performance is conditional — it only has to pay when a covered loss occurs and the policy's conditions have been satisfied.

Covered loss
+ Policy conditions satisfied
→
Insurer performs
Memory

A policy can still be valuable even if no claim is ever filed — the promise of protection existed throughout the policy period, whether or not it was ever called on.

Fortuitous events / unequal exchange

Insurance responds to fortuitous — uncertain — events, which is why the exchange between insurer and insured is fundamentally unequal.

Example

Mari pays a ₹20,000 premium. Three things could happen: no loss occurs, and the insurer may pay nothing at all; a small loss occurs, and the insurer pays the covered repair amount; or a large loss occurs, and the insurer may pay far more than the ₹20,000 premium it collected. That unequal exchange is fundamental to how insurance works.

05 — Characteristics 3 & 4

Utmost good faith & adhesion

Utmost good faith

Both sides are expected to deal honestly with each other. The insured should disclose relevant facts, and the insurer should honor its promises fairly.

Misrepresentation

If Mari is asked about her previous driving violations and answers incorrectly, that may amount to a misrepresentation — even if it wasn't intentional. Utmost good faith doesn't require intent to deceive for a problem to arise.

Memory

Insurance contract = honesty is part of the deal.

Contract of adhesion

The insurer generally drafts the standard policy wording, and the insured usually has limited ability to negotiate its terms — they largely just choose to accept it or reject it.

Insurer drafts form
→
Insured chooses accept / reject
→
Adhesion
Memory

Because the insured can't bargain over the wording, courts generally interpret ambiguous policy language in favor of the insured.

06 — Characteristics 5 & 6

Indemnity & nontransferable

Contract of indemnity

Indemnity means restoring the insured to approximately the same financial position held just before the covered loss — not allowing the insured to profit from the loss. Most property and liability policies are contracts of indemnity.

Before loss — ₹10 lakh financial position
↓
Covered loss occurs
↓
Insurance recovery paid
↓
Approximately back to ₹10 lakh position
Exception to note

A valued policy can pay a stated amount when the specified loss occurs, regardless of the property's actual value at the time of loss — subject to the policy's own terms. It's still tied to the contract, just valued differently than typical indemnity.

Nontransferable contract

Insurance is personal to the insured. Mari generally can't just hand her policy to another person, because the insurer selected Mari specifically as the risk it agreed to insure — not whoever might end up holding the paperwork. Transferring the policy to someone else may require the insurer's consent.

09 — Read Before You Assume

Definitions

Definitions explain how important words are used in that specific policy. A word that sounds perfectly ordinary in everyday language — "vehicle," "occurrence," "resident" — may carry a specific, narrower or broader meaning once the policy defines it.

Memory

Never assume the dictionary meaning. Check the policy definition.

10 — The Core Promise

Insuring agreement

The insuring agreement is the insurer's promise — a statement that the insurer will make a payment, or provide a service, under specified circumstances. It's the heart of the policy: everything else exists to define, limit, or condition this one promise.

11 — What's Carved Out

Exclusions

Exclusions state what is not covered. They exist for good reasons:

  1. 1Avoid duplicate coverage with other policies
  2. 2Manage moral hazards
  3. 3Exclude deliberate losses
  4. 4Manage attitudinal hazards
  5. 5Exclude exposures the insurer doesn't intend to cover

13 — Amending the Deal

Endorsements

An endorsement amends an insurance policy. It can add coverage, remove or change coverage, modify conditions, respond to state requirements, or address changes in the insured's exposure.

Example

Mari buys another vehicle after her auto policy is already in force. An endorsement may modify her policy, per its terms, to reflect the new vehicle rather than requiring an entirely new contract.

15 — Ending Before the Term Is Up

Cancellation

Cancellation means ending a policy before the end of its stated term.

Insured cancellation

The insured can generally request cancellation, following the procedures set out in the policy.

Insurer cancellation

The insurer's ability to cancel is subject to the policy's own provisions and to applicable law, which often restricts when and how an insurer may cancel.

Refund memory

If the insurer cancels → pro rata refund. If the insured cancels and the policy uses a short-rate approach → short-rate refund (less than pro rata, since the insured is initiating the early termination).

Exam Traps

The most important Chapter 7 traps

Trap 1

✗ Insurance contract is only an ordinary contract.

✓ It has the four ordinary contract elements plus special insurance characteristics.

Trap 2

✗ Adhesion means the policy is illegal.

✓ It means the wording is generally drafted by one party and accepted by the other.

Trap 3

✗ Indemnity means insurer always pays market value.

✓ Payment depends on the policy's valuation provisions.

Trap 4

✗ Policy can freely be transferred.

✓ Generally wrong — insurance is personal to the insured and transfer generally requires insurer consent.
